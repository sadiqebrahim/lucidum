The dentist says "no fillings", and you feel wonderful. Yet nothing good actually happened: you're just as healthy as when you walked in. A [new theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26350) explains why that relief feels so real, and it turns on a single idea: feelings track change, not state.

## Feelings start inside the body

Affect, the good-or-bad quality of what we feel, is widely thought to depend on interoception, the sense of the body's internal state: hunger, energy reserves, signals from receptors, even forecasts of what's coming.

## A rate of change

David Balduzzi proposes that valence, how good or bad something feels, is <mark>the rate at which interoception moves a self-sustaining system's responsiveness</mark> relative to its "terminal set", which roughly means death. He formalises responsiveness mathematically, as the scale of the system's Jacobian, a measure of how strongly it can respond.

In plain terms: you feel good when your internal signals say your capacity to keep going is rising, and bad when it's falling. What you feel is the direction and speed of travel, not the position.

## Relief, hunger and pain

Measured on a logarithmic scale, the theory accounts for a range of familiar experiences: pleasure that depends on need, the diffuse feeling of hunger compared with the localised feeling of pain, negativity bias (losses weigh more than equal gains), and relief when an expected threat fails to arrive.

![A line of expected decline and the actual path, which bends upward: the change of direction is felt as relief.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-10-21-relief-valence/relief.png' | relative_url }})
*Relief: the threat you expected never came, and the change of direction is what you feel (schematic).*

Changes in responsiveness that bypass interoception, such as brain damage, aren't felt at all.

## Puzzles from a model body

In a minimal model of a body, the theory reproduces congenital insensitivity to pain, pain asymbolia (feeling pain without it bothering you) and phantom pain. It also shows hunger with full reserves when the reserve signal is driven, and the suppression of pain as energy runs low.

And one striking claim: language models have no valence, because they aren't self-sustaining systems.

## The catch

This is pure theory in a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. There are no experiments on people or animals yet, and the model body is a minimal simulation. Explaining many phenomena with one rule is appealing, but it doesn't show the brain actually works this way.
{: .catch}

Your feelings may be your body's speedometer, not its map.
