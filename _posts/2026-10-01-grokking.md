Train a neural network on a small puzzle and something odd can happen. It gets every training answer right early on, then for a long stretch nothing seems to change. Much later, almost all at once, it starts getting new examples right too. Researchers call this "grokking". A [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38453) offers a theory of why the jump happens.

## Memorise first, understand later

Grokking shows that fitting the training data and learning the rule underneath it can happen at very different times. A model can be perfect on the examples it has seen while still being useless on anything new. The puzzle is what changes in between, when the training score has nothing left to improve.

## The idea: which perfect fit?

Gil Kur, Ileana Rugina, Clémentine Dominé and Marco Mondelli study this in a simplified setting, high-dimensional regression, where many different answers fit the training data exactly. Their point is that the model isn't moving from wrong to right on the training set. It is moving between different perfect fits.

Some of those fits are wiggly: they pass through every training point and still miss the true pattern. Others are simple. The researchers show that a preference for answers with few non-zero numbers (a "sparsity-promoting" norm, such as the L1 norm) is what pulls the model towards the fit that is actually the rule.

## A zero-one law

In strongly overparameterised, noise-free problems, they prove what they call a <mark>zero-one generalisation law</mark>. They build a family of norms whose perfect fits go from the trivial risk of predicting zero everywhere to exact recovery of the rule, while the training error stays at zero the whole time. The answer is either useless or exactly right, with almost nothing in between.

When the number of features and the number of examples grow together, they also map how training and test error change along the path. The benefit of fitting near-perfectly grows as the norm favours sparser answers and as the true rule is sparser.

## Tested beyond the maths

The team checked the predictions on diagonal linear networks and on transformers trained on modular ("clock") arithmetic, a standard grokking test bed. They also flag a side effect: in this regime, small changes to the strength of the regularisation can lead to very different generalisation even when the training error stays small.

## The catch

This is a preprint and hasn't been peer reviewed. The theory is proved for simplified linear models; the transformer experiments show the predictions carry over in one setting, not that they explain grokking in every large model.
