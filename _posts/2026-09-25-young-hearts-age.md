If you move a heart from a young donor into an older recipient, you might hope the new organ keeps some of its youth, and maybe even passes a little of it on. A new preprint from Jesse Poganik and colleagues at Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston suggests it goes the other way. In mice, and in tissue from human transplant patients, the donor heart soon <mark>takes on the biological age of the body it's in</mark>.

## What they did

The team worked in mice first. Rather than swapping out the animal's own heart, they gave each mouse a second one, implanted in the neck, so the new heart and the original could be compared side by side. Some young adult mice got hearts from middle-aged donors; some middle-aged mice got young hearts, [as MIT Technology Review describes it](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/25/1145083/young-organs-may-not-be-a-fountain-of-youth-for-recipients/).

To measure age, they used three "aging clocks": molecular tests that estimate how old a tissue looks biologically, as opposed to how many birthdays it has had. In mice without a transplant, the clocks tracked real age well.

Four to six months after surgery, the transplanted hearts had shifted toward the age of their new owner. Young hearts read older. Old hearts read younger. The recipients' blood, their other organs and their original hearts showed no sign of being changed by the extra heart, whatever its age.

The team then turned to people. Heart transplant patients have repeated small biopsies after surgery, and Brigham and Women's has kept those samples for decades. Run through the same clocks, the human hearts showed the same pattern: whatever the donor's age, the new heart soon matched the recipient's biological age.

## How it works, as far as anyone knows

Nobody knows why yet. João Pedro de Magalhães, an aging researcher at the University of Birmingham who wasn't involved, suggested the recipient's immune cells might be setting the new organ's aging markers as they circulate. Poganik raised two other possibilities: any youthful effect from one young organ may simply be diluted by an older body, or one organ may be too little to matter.

A way to picture it: an organ is less like a sealed time capsule and more like a guest who picks up the habits of the house they move into.

## Why it matters

The idea that young tissue can rejuvenate an old body has a long history in mouse experiments that joined young and old animals' circulations. This result suggests that a single young organ doesn't work that way, at least not for the heart.

It may also matter for transplants. In hospital records, Poganik found that heart recipients were typically around 20 years older than their donors, and hearts from older donors are often turned away on the assumption that they won't work as well. If a heart ends up matching its recipient's age anyway, he hopes surgeons will reconsider some of those older organs.

## The catch

This is a preprint and hasn't been peer-reviewed. Aging clocks measure molecular markers, not how well a heart actually pumps, so "younger" here means younger by those tests. The mouse hearts were extra hearts in the neck, not replacements doing the full job, and the human data come from one hospital's stored samples. The study also doesn't show why the shift happens.
{: .catch}

The short version: a heart seems to answer to the body it's in, not the one it came from.
