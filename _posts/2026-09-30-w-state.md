Checking what kind of entanglement a group of photons shares normally takes a long series of measurements, and the work explodes as photons are added. A team at Kyoto and Hiroshima Universities has now shown how to <mark>identify one important kind in a single shot</mark>, a problem that had stayed open since a similar method for another kind was proposed more than 25 years ago. The paper was published in [Science Advances](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adx4180) in September 2025 and highlighted by [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260929053550.htm) this week.

## One unit of light, shared by three

Entangled photons are linked, so none of them can be described on its own. In a W state, one unit of light (one excitation) is shared by three photons: it is in the first, the second or the third, all at once and in equal parts. Written out, that is

![The W state written as one over root three times the sum of 100, 010 and 001, shown as three rows of three photons with one lit in each.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-30-w-state/w-state.png' | relative_url }})
*|W⟩ = (|100⟩ + |010⟩ + |001⟩)/√3: each term puts the excitation on a different photon.*

## The old way: measure, measure, measure

The standard way to work out which state a set of photons is in is quantum state tomography: measure many copies in many different ways and reconstruct the state from the statistics. The number of measurements needed grows exponentially with the number of photons. In a typical scheme, each extra photon triples the number of measurement settings.

![A graph of measurement settings against the number of photons, rising steeply as three to the power n.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-30-w-state/tomography.png' | relative_url }})
*The cost of tomography grows exponentially (a typical scheme shown).*

## The trick: rotate it, and it looks the same

An entangled measurement skips the reconstruction and asks the question directly, identifying the state in one go. Such a measurement for GHZ states, another family of multi-photon entanglement, was proposed more than 25 years ago, but none existed for W states. The team, led by Shigeki Takeuchi, used a property of W states called cyclic shift symmetry: if you shift the three photons round in a circle, the state looks the same. A photonic circuit that performs a quantum Fourier transform reorganises the photons so that this symmetry reveals which W-type state came in, in a single shot. They demonstrated it with three photons and measured how reliably it gave the right answer.

![Three photons on a ring with the shared light spread over all three; turning the ring leaves the state the same, and an optical circuit reads it in one shot.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-30-w-state/symmetry.png' | relative_url }})
*The symmetry the circuit uses.*

## Why it matters

Quantum teleportation and future quantum networks need quick ways to identify and pass on entangled states. A one-shot measurement for W states, which in principle extends to any number of photons, adds a tool for teleporting multi-photon states, new communication protocols and measurement-based quantum computing.

The demonstration used three photons; larger versions and on-chip circuits are still to come. The paper itself is from 2025 and reached wide attention only this week.

One measurement can now recognise this kind of shared entanglement, a small but real step towards quantum networks.
