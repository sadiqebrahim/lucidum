Senior Associate. Lead Specialist. Vice President at 26. Impressive-sounding job titles seem to be everywhere. A [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26494) asks why firms hand them out so freely, and whether it's good or bad for anyone.

## A promotion is a signal

Since Michael Waldman's work in 1984, economists have understood promotions as signals. When a firm promotes someone, other employers learn something about that person's ability, and may try to hire them away. That makes promotions expensive for the firm: a visible promotion invites outside offers, which pushes up the wage needed to keep the worker.

## Title inflation jams the signal

Yi Chen, Michael Waldman and Zhenda Yin study job title inflation as "signal jamming". If lots of people get senior-sounding titles, a title says less about who's really good. Outside employers can't read the signal as clearly.

*When everyone is "senior", the title stops telling rivals who's really good.*

## Sometimes it helps

In a two-period model, they show that title inflation can arise in equilibrium and can <mark>raise overall welfare</mark>, for two reasons. Workers directly value impressive titles. And jamming the signal reduces inefficiencies in who gets assigned to which job, which promotion signalling otherwise creates.

## Sometimes everyone loses

Then they add a choice for young workers: whether to invest in building their skills. Now the picture flips. When promotions, and the pay rises that come with them, are an important reason to invest in skills, title inflation weakens that incentive. It can lower both overall efficiency and workers' expected well-being, and still persist, because firms keep choosing it.

The paper also looks at extensions with worker turnover, a wage transparency law and differences in how visible workers are to outside employers, and discusses how well the theory matches evidence on real-world title inflation.

## The catch

This is a theoretical model and a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. It shows when title inflation could help or hurt under its assumptions; it doesn't measure how common it is or what it costs in any real labour market. The "VP at 26" example above is our illustration, not a statistic from the paper.
