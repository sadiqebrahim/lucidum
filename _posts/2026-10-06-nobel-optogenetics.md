What if you could switch a single brain cell on or off with a flash of light? That idea, called optogenetics, has [won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://www.hhmi.org/news/karl-deisseroth-2026-nobel-prize-physiology-medicine).

## The winners

The prize goes to Karl Deisseroth of Stanford University, an HHMI Investigator and practising psychiatrist, who shares it with Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University of Berlin and Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg.

## A protein from algae

Neuroscientists long knew that certain brain regions were linked to learning, fear or aggression. What they lacked was a way to switch specific neurons on or off and see what happened, the step needed to show cause and effect.

The answer came from single-celled algae. Hegemann and Nagel discovered a protein called channelrhodopsin. When blue light hits it, a channel in it opens and lets charged particles flow into the cell, producing an electrical signal.

## The leap

Deisseroth's insight was that this algal protein could serve as a light-controlled switch for nerve cells. His team put the channelrhodopsin gene into rat neurons in a dish and showed that <mark>pulses of blue light could make them fire</mark>. He unveiled this in 2005, and within two years had the technique working in living mice.

## What it unlocked

In 2009 Deisseroth used optogenetics to study how deep brain stimulation helps in a model of Parkinson's disease. In 2013 his group showed that light acting on dopamine neurons could quickly reverse the effects of stress-induced depression in animals.

The technique is now used in labs worldwide, in animals from mammals to fish, mosquitoes and the roundworm *C. elegans*, to test which neurons drive which behaviours. HHMI also credits Deisseroth with training many scientists and making the method widely accessible.

## The catch

Optogenetics is mostly a research tool. Its biggest impact so far is on understanding the brain, in animals; HHMI describes it as a step toward eventual treatments for mental health disorders, not a treatment yet. Many other researchers contributed to the field, and a Nobel can go to at most three people.

By borrowing a protein from algae, scientists can flip brain cells like light switches and work out what each one does.
