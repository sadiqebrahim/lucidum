A light-day sounds like a unit of time. It's a distance: how far light travels in 24 hours. [According to NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/where-are-voyager-1-and-voyager-2-now/), on Wednesday 18 November 2026, at 2:16:07 a.m. PST, Voyager 1 will be exactly that far from Earth, the first human-made object ever to get there.

## How far is a light-day?

NASA puts the distance at <mark>16.094 billion miles, or 25.902 billion kilometres</mark>. [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/space/voyager-1-1-light-day-from-earth-november-17-18-2026/) gives it as about 173 astronomical units: 173 times the average distance between Earth and the Sun.

## Still talking after 49 years

Voyager 1 launched on 5 September 1977. EarthSky reports that the trip to one light-day took 49 years, 2 months and 11 days. NASA notes that the two Voyagers are the only spacecraft ever to operate outside the heliosphere, the protective bubble of particles and magnetic fields generated by the Sun.

It is still in contact with Earth, which is the remarkable part.

## Ask on Monday, hear back on Wednesday

At this distance, a radio command from NASA takes about a day to reach the spacecraft, and Voyager's reply takes about a day to come back. Every conversation now has a two-day round trip.

## Running on fading power

Voyager's power supply weakens every year, so NASA has been shutting systems off one after another to keep it going. EarthSky reports that most of the instruments no longer work, and that one particle instrument was switched off in April 2026 to save power.

Its twin, Voyager 2, is about 3.2 billion km closer to Earth. NASA expects it to reach one light-day in November 2035.

## The catch

The milestone is about distance, not a new discovery: Voyager 1 is doing what it has done for decades, just further away. How long it can keep talking depends on how much power is left.
{: .catch}

Our farthest messenger is now a whole day of light away, and still sending news home.
