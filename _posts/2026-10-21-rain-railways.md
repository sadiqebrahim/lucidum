On most rainy days, a few trains run late and that's that. A [new study in PNAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26428) finds that railway networks don't always degrade gently. Past a critical strength of rainfall, the same network can tip from scattered delays into system-wide disruption.

## Rain as a field

Mauro Faccin, Tomas Scagliarini and Manlio De Domenico built a model that couples two things: the dynamics of a transport network, and the dynamics of an external event, treated as a "field" that acts on every part of the network at once. Rain is the obvious example. You can't prevent it, but it affects every station and line it falls on.

## Failures that snowball

Infrastructure can lose function, or fail completely, through a snowball effect of cascading failures. A delay at one point spreads to the next, and if delays pile up faster than they clear, the trouble spreads across the system.

## A tipping point

The team applied the model to real railway networks in Italy and China under rainfall. They found two regimes: local, endemic disruption and system-wide disruption, separated by <mark>a critical value of the rainfall strength</mark>.

![Share of the network delayed against rainfall strength: low and flat, then rising steeply past a critical value.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-10-21-rain-railways/tipping.png' | relative_url }})
*Below the critical rainfall, delays stay local; above it, they go system-wide (schematic).*

They also found that localised perturbations on the wider network reveal local weak points in the global infrastructure, which they quantify as the system's "perturbability" and the corresponding societal risk.

## Why it matters

The authors frame this as a way to quantify critical points in essential infrastructure, at a time when the extreme weather that triggers them is expected to intensify with climate change. The paper also points to the heavy rains of July 2021 in Germany and the Benelux countries: up to 100 mm a day, which affected the functioning of the German-wide rail network.

## The catch

The authors note that the rainfall in their own Italian and Chinese data never reached the disruption phase; the system-wide regime comes from the model, with real-world examples from the literature. This is a model, applied to two countries' networks, and the curves in our video are schematic rather than the paper's data. It identifies a threshold within the model; how close any real network is to its tipping point depends on details such as timetables, repairs and local conditions.
{: .catch}

Railways may not fail gently. They can tip.
