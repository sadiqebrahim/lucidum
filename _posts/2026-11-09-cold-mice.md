Office thermostat wars are a running joke. In many animals, including mice and birds, females prefer slightly warmer temperatures than males. A study in the *Journal of Physiology*, [reported by Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/health/neuroscience/women-often-get-cold-more-easily-than-men-a-mouse-study-offers-hints-why), set out to find why, at least in mice.

## Two plates, one choice

A team including Peter McNaughton of King's College London placed 30 male and 27 female mice on two adjacent plates: one held at 33 °C, the other set cooler or warmer. A camera tracked how long each mouse spent on each plate. The top of the cage was open, so air temperature didn't play a part.

## Same body, different choice

Both sexes avoided the cooler plates. But next to a 38 °C plate, females spent significantly more time on the warmer side. On average, males preferred a surface of about 32 °C and <mark>females about 36 °C</mark>. Yet the researchers found no significant difference in core or skin temperature between the sexes.

## Tuned in the skin

The skin carries sensor proteins in its nerve endings that respond to warmth and to cold. Deleting the warmth sensor made males more willing to stay on warm plates, nudging their preference up to about 35 °C. Blocking the cool sensor reduced cold avoidance in both sexes. As Michael Caterina of Johns Hopkins, who wasn't involved, put it to Live Science, females are a little more responsive to the cold sensor and a little less to the warm one.

## Testosterone, partly

Males given a drug that blocks testosterone production spent more time on the 38 °C plate weeks later. Females given testosterone lost their preference for warmth. Estrogen and progesterone had no effect on the choice. But testosterone didn't close the gap completely, so something else is also involved. How it acts on the sensors isn't known; because the effect took weeks, the researchers suspect an indirect route, perhaps through gene activity.

## The catch

This is a mouse study and wasn't tested in people. The same sensor proteins exist in humans, but an earlier small human study from the US National Institutes of Health found that men and women perceived a cooling room in much the same way.
