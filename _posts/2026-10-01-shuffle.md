Shuffle a deck for long enough and it becomes random. The surprise is how it happens. For many shuffles, randomness doesn't creep in gradually. The deck looks ordered for a long time, then becomes random almost in an instant. A [new proof](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40076) pins down when that happens for one simple shuffle.

## The shuffle

Put *n* cards at the points of a circle. Each pair of neighbours carries its own random clock, which rings on average once per unit of time. When it rings, those two cards swap places. This is the "adjacent transposition shuffle on a cycle". It's slow, because cards only ever move one step at a time.

## A cliff, not a slope

Mathematicians measure how far a deck is from random with the "total variation distance". For many shuffles this distance stays near its maximum for a long time and then falls sharply to near zero. That sharp drop is called a <mark>cutoff</mark>.

![A curve of the distance from random against time, flat at first and then dropping steeply at the cutoff.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-10-01-shuffle/cutoff.png' | relative_url }})
*The deck stays ordered, then turns random within a narrow window (schematic).*

## The exact time

Colin Defant has proved that this shuffle has a cutoff at time

*n*² log *n* / (8π²),

with a window of order at most *n*² log log *n*. That window grows more slowly than the cutoff time itself, so as decks get bigger the switch looks ever sharper compared with the length of the shuffle.

## What it means for 52 cards

Our own arithmetic, not a figure from the paper: for *n* = 52, the formula gives about 135 units of time. A circle of 52 cards has 52 neighbouring pairs, each swapping about once per unit of time, so that's roughly 7,000 swaps. Stop much earlier and the original order is still hiding in the deck. The formula describes large decks, so treat the 52-card number as a guide.

## The catch

This is a preprint and hasn't been peer reviewed. The result is for this particular shuffle on a circle; it doesn't tell you how many riffle shuffles a casino deck needs, which is a different and well-studied question.
{: .catch}

Randomness doesn't creep in. It arrives on schedule, and now we know the schedule.
