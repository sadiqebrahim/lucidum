Pass a rumour down a single street, house to house, and the classic mathematics says it always dies out. A [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02533) finds that letting people forget the rumour can change that, which sounds backwards.

## The classic model

Mathematicians often study rumours with the Maki-Thompson model. Everyone is in one of three states: hasn't heard it, is spreading it, or has heard it and lost interest. When a spreader tells someone who already knows, the spreader gets bored and stops.

Nevena Marić, Aleksandar Minić and Pablo Rodriguez look at this model on the simplest possible network: a line, where everyone can only talk to their neighbours. On a line, spreaders keep running into people who already know, and <mark>the rumour always dies out</mark>.

## Adding forgetting

The team then studied two variants in which spreaders can also forget, a second way to drop out of spreading. You might expect that to kill the rumour even faster. Instead, both variants show a genuine phase transition.

![A curve of the chance the rumour survives forever, zero until a threshold spreading rate, then rising.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-10-06-rumour/threshold.png' | relative_url }})
*Below a critical spreading rate the rumour fizzles; above it, it can last forever (schematic).*

There is a critical transmission rate. Below it, the rumour dies out with certainty. Above it, it survives forever with positive probability. The authors prove that these transitions exist, estimate the critical values, and map how they shift with the "stifling" rate, how quickly spreaders lose interest after meeting someone who knows.

## Why would forgetting help?

The abstract frames it like this: forgetting removes spreaders, yet it can still qualitatively change the dynamics and create regimes of sustained spread. The paper combines rigorous proofs with numerical simulations to explore the phase diagrams. The details of the mechanism are in the full paper; we haven't tried to summarise more than the abstract supports.

## The catch

This is a model on a one-dimensional line, a mathematical idealisation rather than a description of real social networks. The paper has been accepted for publication in *Chaos, Solitons & Fractals*. Some thresholds are estimated with simulations rather than given exactly.
{: .catch}

Forgetting can keep a rumour alive, and there's a measurable tipping point between fizzle and forever.
