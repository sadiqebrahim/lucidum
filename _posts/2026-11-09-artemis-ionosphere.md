When NASA's Artemis II mission launched for the Moon, its rocket left more than exhaust behind. [A new study](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/nasas-artemis-ii-moon-launch-tore-a-hole-in-earths-upper-atmosphere-so-huge-it-disrupted-gps-signals) finds it punched holes thousands of kilometres long in the ionosphere, the electrically charged layer of the upper atmosphere.

## A layer of free electrons

The ionosphere sits from about 50 km up and is full of free electrons. GPS satellites orbit far above it, at about 20,200 km, so every positioning signal has to pass through it. Changes in the number of electrons change how long the signal takes to arrive, and that throws off the position a receiver calculates.

## A shock wave clears the way

As a rocket flies through the high atmosphere, its shock wave effectively sweeps electrons aside and leaves a hole behind it, a bit like a giant contrail.

## Mapped by GPS stations

Researchers at National Cheng Kung University in Taiwan, including Charles Lin, used high-precision networks of ground GPS receivers, normally used to track the slow motion of tectonic plates, to reconstruct how Artemis II's Space Launch System changed the electron content above North America.

They found two holes: one from the core stage, and another from two upper-stage burns fired inside the ionosphere, at around 185 km altitude, more than an hour after launch. Both stretched east to west across the continent, about <mark>5,000 km long</mark>.

![Two long trails across a row of GPS stations: one from the core stage, one from the upper-stage burns, each about 5,000 km long.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-11-09-artemis-ionosphere/holes.png' | relative_url }})
*Two holes, each about 5,000 km long, mapped by ground GPS stations (schematic).*

The disturbances were larger than those from any rocket launch to date, and up to ten times stronger than those after SpaceX's Starship test launches, even though Starship has about twice the liftoff thrust. The upper-stage burns, inside the ionosphere itself, left the more prominent mark.

## Why it matters

The electron changes were equivalent to positioning errors of several metres. Lin told Space.com that a couple of metres is enough to make an autonomous car change lanes, and could affect drones too. With more missions planned to the Moon and Mars, he expects these effects to matter more.

## The catch

This is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. The team inferred the positioning errors from electron changes; it did not directly measure GPS failures, and plans follow-up work on how large and long-lasting any errors were. Rocket effects are short-lived compared with solar storms.
{: .catch}

As more missions head to the Moon and Mars, rockets are becoming a kind of space weather.
