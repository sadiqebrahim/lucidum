You can move in three directions in space, and there's one direction of time, which behaves quite differently. In physics that difference is usually written into a theory from the start. A [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31776) asks whether a theory of quantum gravity could predict it instead.

## One minus, three pluses

In relativity, the "distance" between two events in spacetime is measured with a formula in which the time part carries the opposite sign to the space parts: one minus and three pluses. That pattern is called the metric's signature, and the one-time, three-space version is the Minkowski signature. It's what makes time different from space in the equations.

Most theories simply assume this signature. Mihailo Djordjevic, Igor Prlina and Marko Vojinovic wanted to see whether it could come out of the theory as a prediction.

## Spacetime from tiny flat pieces

They used a toy model based on quantum Regge calculus, one of the "piecewise-flat" approaches to quantum gravity. Spacetime is built from tiny flat four-dimensional building blocks called 4-simplices, the 4-D version of triangles, glued together. The shape of each block is fixed by its edge lengths.

*A 2-D picture of 4-D pieces; shaded pieces stand for those that come out with one time and three space directions (schematic).*

The team ignored the theory's dynamics and looked only at its kinematics: across all the allowed edge lengths, how likely is it that a given block has each possible signature? They computed these probabilities numerically.

## About 85%, with a catch

For a suitably chosen domain of space-like edge lengths and a suitable short-distance cutoff, the probability that a block has the Minkowski signature reaches <mark>about 85%</mark>. The results were essentially the same whatever the topology of spacetime or the way it was cut into blocks.

But the choice matters. When both space-like and time-like edge lengths are allowed, a different "split" signature, two time-like and two space-like directions, dominates instead, with a probability of 72% to 81%.

## Why it matters

The authors argue that the signature doesn't necessarily have to be postulated; it can be predicted, at least in this kind of model. They also estimate how large the cutoff and the range of edge lengths need to be for a model to favour our familiar one time and three space directions, and suggest including the dynamics might raise the probability further.

## The catch

This is a preprint and hasn't been peer reviewed. It's a toy model with no dynamics, and the 85% depends on a choice of edge-length range and cutoff made by the authors; a different, natural-looking choice favours a signature our universe doesn't have.
{: .catch}

Our one time direction might come out of the theory rather than being put in, if the setup is right.
