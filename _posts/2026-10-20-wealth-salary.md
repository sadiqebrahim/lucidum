Inequality is usually measured with fairly involved statistics such as the Gini coefficient. A [new paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24192) argues that a single, simple ratio carries much of the same information: how much wealth there is per person compared with what work pays.

## The ratio

Jhordan Silveira de Borba, Sebastian Gonçalves and Celia Anteneodo call it *R*:

**R = average wealth per person ÷ average salary**

It measures how much accumulated assets matter compared with income from work. When *R* is high, owning things counts for a lot more than earning a wage.

## Where it comes from

In earlier work, the team ran agent-based simulations of a model of a capitalist economy proposed by Ian Wright. They found that inequality in both wealth and income was <mark>largely governed by this one parameter</mark>. Larger values of *R* went with higher Gini coefficients.

![A distribution of people by wealth: as R rises, the middle empties and people pile up at the low and high ends.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-10-20-wealth-salary/middle.png' | relative_url }})
*In the simulations, raising R hollows out the middle (schematic).*

## Does it hold in real economies?

The new paper asks whether the relationship shows up outside the model. Using data from the World Inequality Database and the OECD, the authors built empirical estimates of *R* and compared them with standard measures of income and wealth inequality.

They found a positive relation between *R* and inequality across several settings, strongest in mature, advanced economies. That supports *R* as a simple macroscopic indicator associated with inequality.

## A famous number in disguise

The ratio connects to a quantity economists already track. Our reading of the definitions, not a figure from the abstract: average wealth over average wage is roughly the national wealth-to-income ratio (often written β) divided by labour's share of income. Both are widely studied, which helps explain why *R* behaves the way it does.

## The catch

This is a preprint and hasn't been peer reviewed. The authors stress that differences in how countries and periods define and measure wealth and wages limit the comparisons. A correlation in the data shows *R* is a useful indicator, not that it causes inequality.
