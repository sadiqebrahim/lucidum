Say "ee", then "oo". In that moment your tongue bent through a whole series of shapes. It has no bones and many muscles, so it can take almost any form. How does the brain control something so flexible? A [new study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23456) proposes that it doesn't need to control every detail.

## A high-dimensional problem

Speech production involves an articulatory system with many degrees of freedom. A widespread idea is that speakers manage it through a smaller set of control variables. Popular candidates are individual articulators, such as the tongue body, or abstract "tract variables", such as where along the mouth the tongue body makes its narrowest point. Which coordinate system the brain actually uses is still an open question.

## Whole-tongue shapes

Sam Kirkham and Patrycja Strycharczuk try another candidate: holistic shape modes of the whole tongue. They first found these modes empirically, in a coordinate space shared across speakers, using ultrasound recordings of single vowels from <mark>36 speakers of British English</mark>.

## Springs pulled towards targets

They then fitted a simple dynamical law to how those modes change over time: a three-dimensional linear point attractor. You can picture each mode as a spring pulled towards a target. This model recovered the dynamics of tongue shape with a high degree of accuracy.

The interpretation is that complex tongue movement can come from independent modes that combine linearly under simple laws. Some of the complexity moves out of the control signal and into the coordinate system itself.

## Why single measures look messy

The framing also explains a puzzle. One-dimensional measures, such as tracking a single point on the tongue, can show nonlinear, messy behaviour. If the real control happens in whole-tongue modes, a single point is a mix of several smooth motions, so it can look complicated even when the underlying control is simple.

The authors go further and suggest the shape modes may be a visible, kinematic expression of muscle synergies, groups of muscles working together.

## The catch

This is a preprint and hasn't been peer reviewed. The data cover single vowels from speakers of one language variety. A good model fit shows the modes could serve as a control basis; it doesn't prove that the brain actually uses them. The muscle-synergy link is offered as a hypothesis.
{: .catch}

The brain may steer speech with a handful of simple springs.
