Every so often a headline announces that scientists have frozen light and turned it solid. The most recent wave came with an [Italian study in 2025](https://scienceindiamag.in/italian-scientists-freeze-a-ray-of-light/), which actually made light behave as a *supersolid*, a strange state that is ordered like a crystal yet flows without friction. Nothing in it was stopped or stored. But light really has been brought to a standstill, and the way it was done is worth knowing, because <mark>light never became solid: it was held</mark>.

## Not matter, but energy and information

When people say light was "frozen", it didn't turn into matter. No electrons or protons appeared. What can be trapped is light's energy and the information it carries: the shape and timing of a pulse. Where that information goes, and how it comes back, is the whole trick.

## A cloud made see-through

At Harvard, Lene Hau's team cooled a cloud of sodium atoms to about a billionth of a degree above absolute zero. On its own, a cloud like that absorbs the light that the experiment sends in. A second beam, the control (or coupling) laser, changes that: while it shines, the cloud becomes transparent to the probe light, an effect called electromagnetically induced transparency.

![A cloud of cold sodium atoms, lit by a control laser that makes it see-through.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-29-stopped-light/mirage.png' | relative_url }})
*Schematic: the control laser turns an opaque cloud transparent.*

Inside that transparent cloud the light doesn't simply pass one atom at a time. It spreads through the cloud, sharing itself with the atoms, and it slows down dramatically. In 1999 the team [reported in Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/17561) that light had slowed to 17 metres a second, about the speed of a car in town.

![A light pulse inside the cloud, its speed counter falling from 300,000 kilometres a second to 17 metres a second.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-29-stopped-light/crawl.png' | relative_url }})
*From 300,000 km/s to 17 m/s.*

## How slow, in one line

Light's speed in any material is v = c / n, where c is its speed in empty space and n (strictly, the group index) measures how strongly the material holds it back. Glass holds light back about 1.5 times. Hau's cloud held it back roughly 18 million times.

![The equation v equals c over n, comparing glass at about 1.5 with the cold cloud at about 18 million.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-29-stopped-light/maths.png' | relative_url }})
*Same equation, very different materials.*

## Switch it off, and the light is caught

In 2001 the team [went further](https://doi.org/10.1038/35054017). With a pulse slowed inside the cloud, they switched the control laser off. The transparency vanished and the pulse disappeared, but its shape and phase were [imprinted on the sodium atoms](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2001/01/researchers-now-able-to-stop-restart-light/). The light was, in effect, caught, like a mouse that walks into a cage just before the door shuts. About a thousandth of a second later they switched the laser back on, and the same pulse came back out and left the cloud at full speed. A second Harvard team, at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, [reported storing light in an atomic vapour](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.86.783) the same year.

![A cage closes around the atom cloud; the light's shape stays stored in the atoms.]({{ '/assets/posts/2026-09-29-stopped-light/trapped.png' | relative_url }})
*Laser off: the pulse's shape is held in the atoms until the laser comes back on.*

## Why it matters

Storing light and releasing it on demand is the basic idea behind quantum memories, which future quantum networks will need to hold fragile quantum information while it waits to be passed on. Since these first experiments, researchers have stored light for longer and in solid crystals and chips as well as gases.

These experiments are from 1999 and 2001, not new research; we're telling the story because the "light turned solid" headline keeps resurfacing. The storage time here was about a thousandth of a second, and it required atoms cooled almost to absolute zero.
